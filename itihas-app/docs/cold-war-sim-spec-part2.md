## 11. PACING & FUN LAYER (for high schoolers)

Design goal: an 8-turn game feels like 8 essays. Break each era into **beats** so every 2–3 minutes something happens, and give players people to argue with.

### 11.1 The turn becomes four beats
1. **Headline beat** (10 s): three newspaper front pages slide in — your capital, the enemy capital, a neutral city (e.g., Delhi, Stockholm, Cairo). Same event, three spins. Player taps "Read on."
2. **Cabinet beat** (30 s): four advisors argue. Player can tap each for their one-liner before choosing. Optional: "Vote of confidence" — player predicts which advisor history listened to (+ badge if right).
3. **Decision beat**: the 3 choices from Part 1.
4. **Ripple beat**: the 5–6 map pins. Then a **Flashpoint** (below) fires between turns.

### 11.2 The Cabinet (composite characters, NOT real people)
Four fictional roles, same silhouettes recolored per country. They never quote real leaders. Personalities:
- **The General** — wants force, hates half-measures, quotes battles. Catchphrase: "Hesitation is a decision too."
- **The Diplomat** — wants talks, sees every ally's fears. "Nobody remembers who was right. They remember who was in the room."
- **The Treasurer** — counts money, hates wars, loves trade. "Every tank is a school we didn't build."
- **The Spymaster** — knows secrets, trusts nobody, occasionally wrong. "Here's what they're not telling you…"

Per choice, each advisor gives ≤ 20 words. Claude Code: generate from the choice's effects (Treasurer reads ECON deltas, General reads TENSION, etc.). Two worked samples:

**USA_T4 (Cuba 1962):**
- General → B: "Strike now, before the missiles are live. Every hour we wait, Havana gets stronger."
- Diplomat → A: "Blockade buys time. Give Khrushchev a door — he's looking for one."
- Treasurer → A: "Markets are already down 10%. An invasion means rationing. A blockade means a bad week."
- Spymaster → (warning): "We count 40 launchers. We do *not* know if the warheads are there yet." *(Truth: they were.)*

**USSR_T7 (Afghanistan 1979):**
- General → A: "Three months. Kabul, the ring road, done. The Afghans don't fight organized armies."
- Diplomat → B: "Détente survives if we stay out. Invade and Carter loses to a cowboy."
- Treasurer → B: "The harvest failed. We can buy wheat or buy a war. Not both."
- Spymaster → A: "Amin met the Americans twice. If we don't move, they will." *(Truth: exaggerated; the KGB over-read the contacts.)*

### 11.3 Flashpoints (8 shared quick beats, between turns)
Two-option, 15-second events that break up the big decisions. Every country sees them; text adapts with `{country}` tokens. One effect each; small meter deltas.

| After turn | Flashpoint | Option 1 | Option 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **1949 — "Joe-1."** The Soviets test a bomb years early. | Tell the public now (STAB −4, INFL +3) — *pin: Los Alamos: crash H-bomb program* | Hide it 3 months (STAB +2, INFL −2) — *pin: Washington: leaks anyway; trust drops* |
| 2 | **1957 — Sputnik.** A beeping ball orbits over every capital. | Fund science education & a space program (ECON −4, INFL +6) — *pin: Huntsville / Baikonur: the space race* | Shrug — "it's a stunt" (ECON +2, INFL −5) — *pin: your capital: a "missile gap" panic* |
| 3 | **1960 — U-2 shot down.** A spy plane is caught over Sverdlovsk. | Admit it (TENSION +5, INFL +2) — *pin: Paris summit collapses* | Deny it and get caught (TENSION +8, STAB −3) — *pin: Moscow: Khrushchev humiliates the West* |
| 4 | **1967 — Six-Day War.** Israel destroys three Arab air forces in a week. | Back your side hard (TENSION +6) — *pin: Cairo or Tel Aviv: patron ties tighten* | Push a UN ceasefire (TENSION −3, INFL +2) — *pin: New York: Resolution 242* |
| 5 | **1969 — Moon landing.** | Broadcast it live (INFL +4 if US-aligned; STAB −3 if Soviet) — *pin: Sea of Tranquility* | Hide/downplay it (STAB +1, INFL −3) — *pin: your capital: everyone hears anyway* |
| 6 | **1973 — Oil shock.** Prices quadruple in 3 months. | Ration & fund alternatives (STAB −5, ECON +3 long-term) — *pin: Riyadh: OPEC learns its power* | Borrow & subsidize (STAB +3, ECON −6) — *pin: your capital: inflation era* |
| 7 | **Sept 1983 — Petrov's night.** A Soviet early-warning system reports 5 US missiles inbound. | *(Not a choice — a cutscene.)* Lt. Col. Petrov decides it's a false alarm. TENSION −10. *pin: Serpukhov-15: one man, one decision* | — |
| 8 | **1986 — Chernobyl.** | Tell the world within 24 h (STAB −6, INFL +5) — *pin: Pripyat / Stockholm: Sweden detects it first* | Cover up (STAB +2 then −10 next beat) — *pin: Kyiv: May Day parade proceeds under fallout* |

Petrov is deliberately a non-choice: the lesson is that history sometimes turned on people who were *not* in charge.

### 11.4 Butterfly Cards (collectibles)
Every effect pin, once tapped, becomes a card in the **Butterfly Album** (place, consequence, insight, turn, your choice). 48 per playthrough max. Album persists across playthroughs → students collect the *counterfactual* butterflies by replaying. Rarity = how few paths reach that effect. Sets: "Berlin" (all 12 Berlin effects), "The Global South," "Nuclear Near-Misses."

### 11.5 Badges (12)
- **Arkhipov Award** — TENSION ≥ 80 and you chose the de-escalating option.
- **Historian** — ≥ 7/8 historical choices.
- **Chaos Theorist** — 0/8 historical choices and survived.
- **Butterfly Collector** — tapped every pin in a game.
- **Passport** — finished as 3 different countries.
- **Perspective** — same era, played from both sides (e.g., USA_T4 and USSR_T4).
- **Long Game** — reached E19 or E13.
- **Cassandra** — correctly predicted the historical advisor 6+ times.
- **Treasurer's Friend** — finished with ECON ≥ 80.
- **No Man's Land** — finished with TENSION ≤ 20.
- **Non-Aligned** — earned E16.
- **Debrief Reader** — opened all 3 discussion questions (teachers love this one).

### 11.6 Vocabulary taps
Underlined terms open a 25-word popup: containment · détente · proxy war · MAD · non-alignment · sphere of influence · domino theory · brinkmanship · glasnost · Ostpolitik · hegemon · deterrence. Claude Code: static JSON, ~15 terms.

### 11.7 Classroom "Council" mode
4 students = 4 advisors on one screen; each reads their line aloud; class votes; teacher advances. Same content, zero new writing.

### 11.8 Tone rules for all new text
Second person, present tense, short sentences, one vivid concrete detail per situation ("The Gloucesters hold a hill for three days with no water"). Never sarcastic about deaths. Jokes live in advisor lines and badge names, not in casualty lines.

## 12. WEST GERMANY (FRG) — Start: STABILITY 45 · ECON 30 · INFLUENCE 25
**Intro:** It's 1948. Your cities are rubble, your country is split in four, your neighbors hate you, and the word "Germany" makes everyone reach for a rifle. You have one asset: everybody needs you. The Americans need a shield, the French need a market, the Soviets need you not to be in NATO. You're not sovereign yet. Play it right and you'll be Europe's richest country in twenty years. Play it wrong and you're a battlefield.

Turn note: Turn 1 spans 1948–49 (currency reform, Basic Law); Turn 3 is 1955–57 (NATO, EEC) rather than Suez.

**FRG_T1 · 1948–49 · "A State Half Built"** — The West wants a separate West German state. Adenauer says yes; Schumacher's Social Democrats fear it locks in division. Stalin is blockading Berlin.
- A. Found the Federal Republic, Face West *(hist)* Δ STAB+8 ECON+10 INFL+6 · Bonn(50.74,7.10): Basic Law, May 1949; a "provisional" capital that lasts 41 years — a state built to be temporary and outlived its purpose · East Berlin(52.52,13.40): The GDR is founded in October in reply — two Germanies, each claiming to be the real one · Frankfurt(50.11,8.68): The Deutsche Mark launches the Wirtschaftswunder — a currency became a national identity · Paris(48.86,2.35): France, terrified of a revived Germany, proposes the Coal and Steel Community — European integration starts as a leash on you · Moscow(55.76,37.62): Stalin lifts the blockade — he lost Berlin and gained a permanent grievance.
- B. Refuse a Separate State — Demand All-German Elections · Δ STAB−5 ECON−6 INFL+4 · flag `FRG_NEUTRAL` · Berlin: Four-power talks drag for years; no currency reform in the East — the whole country stays poor longer · Washington(38.9,−77.04): The US, needing a Western anchor, builds it in France and Italy instead — Germany is a bystander in NATO's founding · Moscow: Stalin dangles a neutral unified Germany (as he actually did in 1952) — this branch makes it real · Bonn: Adenauer loses to Schumacher — a Social Democratic, neutralist West Germany · Vienna(48.21,16.37): Austria and Germany become twin neutral states by 1955 — a "Central European zone" the Rapacki Plan dreamed of.
- C. Found the State AND Demand Immediate Rearmament · Δ STAB−4 ECON+6 INFL−8 · Paris: France's Assembly explodes; the EDC is stillborn in 1949, not 1954 — European integration is set back five years · Moscow: Stalin cites German rearmament to justify a permanent army in the GDR — the Cold War's front line hardens early · Bonn: Veterans' groups and old officers rush in — denazification collapses faster · Washington: Truman refuses; the request embarrasses the occupiers · London(51.51,−0.13): British Labour recoils — Britain distances from the German project for a decade.

**FRG_T2 · 1952 · "The Stalin Note"** — Stalin offers unification: a neutral, armed Germany with free elections, out of NATO. Adenauer says it's a trap; millions of Germans want to take it. The US wants you in a European army.
- A. Reject the Note, Join the West *(hist)* Δ STAB+3 ECON+6 INFL+8 · Bonn: Adenauer wins 1953 on "no experiments" — Western integration becomes the FRG's DNA · Moscow: Stalin dies in 1953; the offer dies with him — historians still argue if it was real · East Berlin: The June 1953 workers' uprising is crushed by Soviet tanks — East Germans learn nobody is coming · Paris: The European Defense Community passes the German Bundestag, then dies in the French Assembly in 1954 — NATO membership follows in 1955 · Washington: The US commits 250,000 troops to Germany for 40 years — you got a guarantee by giving up unity.
- B. Accept the Note — Test It · Δ TENSION−10 STAB−8 ECON−3 INFL+10 · flag `FRG_NEUTRAL` · flag `EARLY_REUNIFICATION` (50%) · Berlin: Four-power talks produce either a united neutral Germany by 1955 (historians' "what if") or a collapse that leaves you looking naïve · Washington: Eisenhower is furious; NATO loses its front line — the US considers withdrawing to the Rhine · Moscow: Even if sincere, Stalin's successors must agree — Beria (who actually floated a deal) is shot in 1953 · Warsaw(52.23,21.01): Poland, facing a united Germany, clings harder to Moscow — the Oder-Neisse border becomes the price · Paris: France demands guarantees; the Franco-German reconciliation is delayed a decade.
- C. Counter-Offer: Unification Inside a Neutral Central Europe (Germany + Poland + Czechoslovakia) · Δ TENSION−5 INFL+6 · Warsaw: Poland's leaders are intrigued; Moscow vetoes — but the idea survives as the Rapacki Plan (1957) · Moscow: Stalin's offer was for Germany only — he refuses; the note's sincerity is exposed either way · Washington: Dulles calls it "neutralism," his least favorite word — US aid tightens · Bonn: Adenauer's coalition splits; a "third way" party emerges · Vienna: Austria's 1955 State Treaty is cited as proof neutrality works.

**FRG_T3 · 1955–57 · "Sovereign"** — You're in NATO, sovereign, and offered the Treaty of Rome (EEC). The Hallstein Doctrine says you'll break relations with anyone who recognizes East Germany. Should you also want nuclear weapons?
- A. Join the EEC, Enforce Hallstein, No Own Nukes *(hist)* Δ ECON+12 INFL+8 · Rome(41.9,12.5): Treaty of Rome, 1957 — the Common Market makes you Europe's engine · Belgrade(44.79,20.45): Yugoslavia recognizes the GDR in 1957; you cut ties — Hallstein costs you the Third World · Paris: De Gaulle and Adenauer reconcile (1963 Élysée Treaty) — the Franco-German engine · Bonn: Adenauer wants "tactical" nukes anyway; the US says no, deploys its own under dual-key — Germany hosts the bombs it may not own · East Berlin: Ulbricht, denied recognition, tightens the border; 200,000 flee yearly — the pressure that builds the Wall.
- B. Join the EEC AND Pursue a Franco-German-Italian Bomb (the real 1957 secret talks) · Δ TENSION+8 ECON−5 INFL+6 · Paris: De Gaulle takes power in 1958 and cancels the joint program (as he did) — or, at 40%, doesn't: a European bomb by 1965 · Washington: The US threatens to withdraw troops; Kennedy later offers the MLF to bury the idea · Moscow: Khrushchev's 1958 Berlin ultimatum comes harder — "German fingers on the trigger" is his best propaganda · Warsaw: Poland's Rapacki Plan gains Western supporters — nuclear-free zone talks · Bonn: The SPD's "fight atomic death" campaign brings 1.5 million into the streets.
- C. Drop Hallstein — Recognize Everyone, Trade With the East · Δ STAB−3 ECON+8 INFL+4 · East Berlin: Recognition stabilizes the GDR; fewer flee; the Wall might never be needed — or comes anyway · Belgrade: Third World ties bloom; German exports to Asia and Africa double by 1965 · Washington: Dulles is appalled; US–German trust dips · Bonn: Adenauer's CDU splits; Ostpolitik arrives 12 years early · Moscow: Khrushchev pockets the recognition and asks for more — Berlin is next.

**FRG_T4 · 1961 · "The Wall"** — East Germany is bleeding 2,000 people a day through Berlin. On August 13 barbed wire goes up overnight. Kennedy sends a battle group but won't tear it down. West Berliners feel abandoned. Adenauer stays in Bonn.

- B. - C. Negotiate Directly With the GDR — "Small Steps" · Δ TENSION−4 STAB+2 INFL+3 · Berlin: A 1963 pass agreement lets West Berliners visit the East at Christmas (this was real) — expanded into a permanent arrangement · East Berlin: Ulbricht, gaining a channel, loosens some travel — the Wall gets doors · Washington: The US worries you're going neutral; aid is tied to loyalty · Moscow: Khrushchev sees a wedge; offers a German peace treaty · Warsaw: Poland asks for the same channel — Eastern Europe's people-to-people contacts start early.

**FRG_T5 · 1966–69 · "The Grand Coalition and the Streets"** — Recession, the far-right NPD in state parliaments, students in the streets, an emergency-powers law. Kiesinger (a former Nazi party member) leads with Brandt as foreign minister. Prague Spring is crushed next door.

## 13. CUBA — Start: STABILITY 40 · ECON 30 · INFLUENCE 20
**Intro:** It's 1953. You're 26, a lawyer, and you've just decided to attack an army barracks with 130 friends. Cuba is a casino for Americans and a sugar plantation for their companies; Batista took power in a coup. Ninety miles away sits the most powerful country on earth. You will play the revolution — and then the state it builds. Every choice will be watched from Washington and Moscow. Choose how small a country stands up.

Turn note: Cuba's timeline shifts (1953 → 1991). Effects still write to global flags.

*Discuss:* Is economic strength the "real" weapon of a long rivalry? · What did the US and its allies spend on the Cold War, and what else could it have bought? · What kept the West from this ending historically?

**E08 · Red Dawn Over Europe** (80)
*Card:* Poland was crushed by foreign tanks; the Wall stood; Western Europe's peace movements chose neutrality over missiles. By 1990 the Iron Curtain had moved west in spirit if not in territory, and Moscow's hardliners — never reformers — ruled a sullen, armed, poor empire that endured.
*Reality — counterfactual.* Poland's martial law in 1981 was imposed by Poles, not Soviets; Western Europe deployed the missiles despite the protests; and the USSR chose a reformer in 1985. Each was a close-run decision.
*Discuss:* Why did Moscow let Polish generals do the job in 1981? · Was Western Europe's peace movement naïve, wise, or both? · What would a Soviet Union led by hardliners have looked like in 2000?

**E09 · Fortress America** (78)
*Card:* You brought the troops home in 1947 and never sent them back. Europe went its own way — some of it east. Asia armed itself. America was safe, rich, and alone, watching a world it had declined to shape. The Cold War happened; you were not in it.
*Reality — counterfactual.* Isolationism was the strongest tradition in American foreign policy until 1941 and remained powerful in 1947 — the Marshall Plan passed only after the Czech coup frightened Congress. Historians argue about what a US-less Europe would have looked like; few think it would have been freer.
*Discuss:* Is withdrawal a decision or an absence of one? · What did the US get — and give up — by staying in Europe for 45 years? · Could a country be "safe" while the rest of the world is not?

**E10 · The Eurasian Bloc** (76)
*Card:* Moscow and Beijing healed their split and never talked to Washington. A single communist landmass from Berlin to Hanoi, 1.2 billion people, two nuclear arsenals, one enemy. The West's "triangle" never existed; the world was a line, and everyone stood on one side of it.
*Reality — counterfactual.* The Sino-Soviet alliance of 1950 lasted barely a decade, broken by ideology, ego, and the border. Nixon's 1972 opening exploited a split that already existed. Whether the alliance could have been repaired after 1969 is one of the great "what ifs."
*Discuss:* Why did two communist giants hate each other more than they hated the West? · How much of the West's Cold War strategy depended on the split? · What does the modern China–Russia relationship owe to this history?

**E11 · Iron Curtain Holds** (74)
*Card:* No glasnost. No Wall coming down. Leipzig's marchers were shot, the Baltics were held by tanks, and the Soviet Union entered the 21st century as a nuclear-armed, shrinking, stubborn state — a North Korea the size of a continent. Nobody won. Nobody lost. Everybody waited.
*Reality — counterfactual, very close.* Gorbachev's selection in March 1985 was a Politburo vote. In October 1989 East German troops in Leipzig had live ammunition and orders that were never given. In January 1991 Soviet tanks did kill 14 people in Vilnius — and then stopped. The peaceful ending was chosen, repeatedly, by people who could have chosen otherwise.
*Discuss:* Why do we assume the USSR "had to" fall? · What would daily life in a 1999 East Germany be like? · Can force hold an empire together indefinitely — and at what cost?

**E12 · Two Roads to Market** (72)
*Card:* The USSR did what China did: markets, no ballots. By 2000 two authoritarian capitalist giants faced a democratic West that had never planned for rivals who were rich. The Cold War did not end in 1991. It changed uniforms.
*Reality — counterfactual.* Kosygin's 1965 reforms and China's 1978 reforms were the same idea; the USSR abandoned it, China did not. Whether a Soviet "Chinese path" could have worked — with a far more industrialized, urban economy and a bloc to subsidize — is fiercely debated.
*Discuss:* Why did China's reforms succeed where Soviet ones failed? · Is a market economy possible without political freedom? Look at the evidence. · Is this ending better or worse than the historical one — and for whom?

**E13 · The Grand Bargain** (70)
*Card:* The West bailed Moscow out. A reforming, confederal Soviet Union survived into the 1990s — no Yeltsin, no oligarchs, no fifteen new states, no Ukrainian independence, no NATO expansion, no 2022. Also no self-determination for 100 million people who never got to vote on their country. This is the ending where "better" depends on who's asking.
*Reality — counterfactual, proposed.* In 1991 the "Grand Bargain" (~$30 billion in aid for reform) was pitched to Bush and rejected. Germany paid Gorbachev DM 15 billion for reunification. Whether money could have saved the union is unclear; the republics were already leaving.
*Discuss:* Should the West have paid to keep the USSR together? · Whose interests would that have served — and whose wouldn't it? · How do you weigh stability against self-determination?

**E14 · The Early Thaw** (68)
*Card:* Hungary or Czechoslovakia was allowed to breathe — and the bloc did not collapse. A neutral belt formed in Central Europe; the arms race slowed; the Cold War thawed twenty years early into something colder than peace but warmer than war. The 1970s felt like the 1990s.
*Reality — counterfactual.* Austria (1955) proves a neutral state next to the bloc was possible; Finland proves a tolerated one was. Nagy in 1956 and Dubček in 1968 asked for versions of this and were crushed. Whether Moscow could have tolerated it without the whole empire unraveling is the question.
*Discuss:* Why did Moscow accept a neutral Austria but not a neutral Hungary? · Would a thaw in 1956 have made 1989 unnecessary — or impossible? · What does "neutral" cost a small country?

**E15 · The Pacific Century** (66)
*Card:* China reformed its economy *and* its politics, and did not shoot its students. By 2000 it was rich, roughly free, and the center of gravity of the world economy. The Cold War's last decade was about Beijing, not Moscow, and the 21st century arrived early.
*Reality — counterfactual.* Zhao Ziyang's faction argued for dialogue in May 1989 and lost; Zhao spent 15 years under house arrest. China's economic rise happened anyway; its political opening did not. Historians disagree sharply about whether the two could have gone together.
*Discuss:* Why did China's leaders conclude the USSR's mistake was *political* opening? · What does a "democratic China" scenario assume, and are the assumptions fair? · How has Tiananmen shaped how the world sees China today?

**E16 · The Third Way Wins** (64)
*Card:* Non-alignment was not a dodge; it was a plan. India, Egypt, Yugoslavia, and dozens of new nations built a bloc that made both superpowers bid for its favor — and got development instead of proxy wars. The Cold War was still fought, but the Global South declined to be its battlefield.
*Reality — partly historical.* The Non-Aligned Movement (1961) was real and at times powerful — at the UN, over Suez, in decolonization. But it was undercut by its members' own alignments (India–USSR, Cuba–USSR) and by superpower interventions from the Congo to Afghanistan. Its "victory" is a counterfactual; its existence is not.
*Discuss:* Was non-alignment principled or opportunistic — and can it be both? · Why did the superpowers find it so threatening? · Where is the NAM today, and does it matter?

**E17 · Proxy Wars Without End** (62)
*Card:* No nuclear war — just every other kind. Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan, Central America, the Horn of Africa: the superpowers never touched each other and burned everyone else. The Cold War did not end in 1991 in this world; it simply ran out of new places to fight.
*Reality — historical, in a sense.* Around 20 million people died in Cold War proxy conflicts. Your ending exaggerates the pattern but does not invent it. The difference is that the historical rivalry eventually exhausted one side; yours kept both fed.
*Discuss:* Who paid the Cold War's real death toll? · Why is "no direct war" counted as success? · Pick one proxy war and trace its causes back to a superpower decision in this game.

**E18 · The Quiet Dividend** (60)
*Card:* No Vietnam. No Afghanistan. Two wars that never happened, two generations who came home because they never left, and two superpowers that spent the money elsewhere. The Cold War ended anyway — a bit later, a bit quieter, and without the wounds that shaped the 1970s and the 2000s.
*Reality — counterfactual.* Both interventions were argued against at the time — by George Ball in Washington (1965) and Alexei Kosygin in Moscow (1979). Historians generally agree the US could have avoided Vietnam without losing the Cold War; they are less sure the USSR could have avoided Afghanistan without losing Central Asia's confidence.
*Discuss:* What made Vietnam and Afghanistan seem necessary to the people who chose them? · What would each country have done with the money and the lives? · Which "quiet" decisions in history deserve more credit?

**E19 · 1991: The Long Peace** (58)
*Card:* Forty-four years, no direct war between the superpowers, and one December night the red flag comes down over the Kremlin. You made most of the calls the real leaders made. Whether that's wisdom or luck is the question this whole game is about.
*Reality — historical.* The Cold War ended not with a battle but with a bankruptcy, a botched press conference, and a general secretary who would not order his soldiers to fire. Around 20 million died in its proxy wars anyway — in Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, Angola, Central America.
*Discuss:* Was the peaceful ending inevitable, or a near miss? · Who "won"? Ask a Vietnamese, a Pole, and an Angolan. · Which of your choices would you make differently knowing what you know now?

**E20 · Frozen Peace** (0, fallback)
*Card:* Nothing broke. Nothing healed. The Wall stands or falls; the missiles stay or go; the two blocs glare at each other into a new century, neither strong enough to win nor weak enough to quit. Your path was careful and strange, and it produced a world that simply… continued.
*Reality — counterfactual, but the most common "expected" future in 1985.* Almost no one in 1985 predicted the Cold War would end within six years. Analysts on both sides assumed a permanent standoff. Your ending is what the experts thought would happen.
*Discuss:* Why did almost nobody see 1989 coming? · Is a "frozen" peace a success or a failure? · What sudden changes in today's world might experts be missing?
